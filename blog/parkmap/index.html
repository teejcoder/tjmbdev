Building parkmap.melbourne

City of Melbourne publishes vast amounts of data for analysis and development. One of those datasets exposes the on-street parking bays scattered throughout the city.

I thought it would be useful to build something for the public that takes that data and renders a live map with a history and forecast.
The map answers these questions: "What street has open bays, how hard is it to park there, where can I park on x day at y time".

A technical deepdive of parkmap.melbourne - A near-real-time map of on-street parking.

What it is

parkmap.melbourne is a map exposing parking bays across Melbourne from thousands of in-ground parking bay sensors across the city. Click a bay and you get its status, its zone's restriction schedule, and a short list of better options nearby. Scrub backwards and you get seven days of history, played back like a recording. Look forward and you get a probabilistic forecast of how likely a given street is to have a free bay within the next 48 hours, built from the data the sensors have already produced.

It is not a reservation system. It tells you what the most recent sensor reading says, how old that reading is, and lets you decide how much to trust it. This is the actual design principle the whole thing is built around.

This is a portfolio piece: something built to be legible to other devs in about five minutes, and to demonstrate that I can take a messy, half-documented government data feed and turn it into something that's honest about its own limitations.

Day one: the API

The City of Melbourne publishes its parking sensor data as an open feed — GeoJSON, updated roughly every 2 minutes. It's free to use under a CC BY 4.0 licence.
Reading the field names, one looked completely unambiguous: status_description, with two possible values, "Present" and "Unoccupied". You'd reasonably assume "Present" means a car is present, i.e. the bay is occupied. You'd also reasonably assume the opposite framing — that the field is answering "is a car present in this reading," full stop, which is exactly what it does. The gotcha wasn't that the field lied; it's that reading it quickly and confidently, the way you read a field you're sure you understand, gets you the wrong answer. is_free is status_description == "Unoccupied", and if you build the query the "obvious" way, you get a map showing occupied bays as free and empty bays as taken — the single worst possible bug for a parking map to ship.

There's a second, quieter data-integrity gap in the same feed, and this one isn't really a bug — it's a boundary the data itself draws, and the honest move is to respect it rather than paper over it. The sensors that report whether a bay is occupied identify each bay one way (a kerbsideid); the separate feed listing what each bay's actual signage restricts — 1P, 2P, loading zone, permit — identifies bays a different way (bayid). The City of Melbourne never publishes a link between the two IDs. There is no way, from the data that exists, to say "this exact bay is 2P until 6pm" — any app that displays that would be guessing and presenting the guess as fact. What the data does support is coarser but real: bays carry a zone number, and zones link reliably (for roughly 90% of them) to the actual signage rules for that area. So the map shows a zone-level hint instead of a bay-specific claim — "this area commonly shows 2P, check the sign" — which is less satisfying than a precise answer but is the one the data can actually back up.

The other founding fact from that first week: roughly half the sensor network was silently dead. Not slowly degrading, but bimodal. A sensor either reported fresh data or it hadn't spoken in weeks, with almost nothing in between. That fact became a permanent constraint on the whole UI. The map doesn't report a bare percentage of free bays, because a bare percentage implies a denominator you can trust. It says "based on X of Y bays" — always. Coverage is treated as a feature to disclose, not a caveat to bury in a footnote.

The stack, and the one part I over-built

The technical shape of the project is fairly conventional: Next.js on Vercel for the frontend, Supabase (Postgres + PostGIS) for the database, Deno Edge Functions on pg_cron for ingestion, MapLibre GL for rendering. None of that is where the story is. The interesting reversal is the basemap.

Rendering a detailed vector map of a city center means either paying a per-request tile provider or hosting your own tiles. My first plan was the "proper" version of self-hosting: use Protomaps' open tile format, but put the tile archive in a Cloudflare R2 bucket, with its own CORS configuration and its own account to manage. It's the textbook scalable answer. It's also the wrong answer at this scale. The actual tile extract for the Melbourne CBD, cut down to just the area the app needs, is 7.2MB. That fits inside a Next.js app's public/ folder as an ordinary static file, served by Vercel exactly like any other asset, with zero extra infrastructure, zero extra accounts, and zero additional failure modes. The R2 plan was dropped once that became obvious — a small, satisfying instance of building the sophisticated version first and then realising the simple version was correct.

How fast is "live," actually

"Live" is a word that invites a specific assumption — that the dot on the map flips the instant a car pulls out. It doesn't, and the honest number is worth stating plainly: worst case, the map can run a bit over two minutes behind reality. That's not a corner cut carelessly; it's a floor set upstream, by the data source itself, and the interesting part is how that got confirmed.

A user once reported the map feeling roughly three minutes stale. That's the right kind of complaint to get, because it prompts a measurement. The investigation found three separate delays stacked on top of each other:

  1. How often the City of Melbourne's own feed republishes.
  2. How often this app's own background job checks that feed.
  3. How long the app briefly caches a response before checking again.

The first of those was checked directly by polling the raw government feed every 20 seconds and watching a timestamp shared across thousands of sensors hold perfectly still, then jump by exactly two minutes, on schedule. That's a clean, confirmed floor: the source itself only republishes every two minutes, full stop.

Two of the three delays were pure overhead — waiting stacked on top of the real floor for no benefit — and got tightened down. The two-minute government-side floor is the one thing that can't be improved from this side at all, and deliberately wasn't touched: polling the source feed faster wouldn't produce fresher data, since the feed isn't updating any faster than every two minutes no matter how often it's asked. It would just mean roughly four times the requests and four times the stored history, for a number on screen that would look exactly the same. Confirming that before "fixing" it saved a chunk of infrastructure.

Making the map look honest

The visual design of the map went through more failed attempts than I expected, and the failures weren't really about taste, they were about the same "looked fine in isolation but wrong in context" pattern showing up in pixels.

Here's an early design draft



The map uses a dark basemap by default — a risky choice, since saturated accents on dark backgrounds are a known anti-pattern ("neon on black"). The first palette looked fine swatch-to-swatch but rendered as solid black on the actual map: buildings sat at roughly ΔE 9 against the background, well under what a human eye reliably distinguishes. Overcorrecting, brightened roads then multiplied across thousands of line segments into a glaring white wireframe that outcompeted the dots it was supposed to support. The fix pushed building contrast to ΔE ~23 while keeping roads restrained, and gave parks, water, and motorways their own muted hues (olive, teal, sand) so nothing competed with the dot palette. Obvious once wrong in a screenshot; invisible comparing colors two at a time in a palette editor.

The dot colours have to work as a claim: free is the only fully saturated color (the one thing worth acting on), occupied is dimmer, stale is faded almost to nothing. Color is never the only signal — brightness and a glow ring carry the same meaning in parallel, and a dedicated colour-blind mode swaps in a blue/red/yellow palette used identically across live, rewind, and forecast.

The search-radius ring is drawn as a real geographic circle, sized in ground metres, rather than the map engine's default pixel-radius circle — which would mean a different real-world distance depending on zoom. Same failure mode as everything else here: looks correct until you check what it's actually claiming.

Three ways to look at the map

The map has three different views, each answers a different question.

Live shows the most recent sensor reading for every bay, with its freshness checked right at the moment it's drawn.

Rewind plays back the last seven days of observed activity like a recording — drag a scrubber and watch actual history replay across the whole CBD. It deliberately uses the exact same colors as the live view rather than the forecast's gradient, because it's showing things that genuinely happened.

Forecast looks forward instead of back: pick a future hour, and every street is colored by the probability of finding a free bay there at that time, based on how that street has actually behaved historically at that hour of the week. It uses a visibly different gradient-style palette specifically so it can never be mistaken for a live reading at a glance — and where a street's historical data is too thin to trust, the map says so outright in grey rather than rendering a confident-looking guess.

Building a forecast without pretending it's a prediction

Once enough history had accumulated, the natural next step was a forecast layer — an "arrive at 5pm, how likely is a bay nearby" view. The build order here is worth mentioning: an early implementation tried to answer this with a probe-grid approach, effectively cross-joining candidate time probes against every historical interval. It's conceptually simple and it's O(probes × intervals), which is fine until the tables grow, at which point it just times out. The replacement — duration-weighted interval-splitting, computing P(free) directly from how long each bay actually spent in each state — was strictly better on every axis: faster, simpler to reason about, and cheaper to run.

The forecast layer also inherited a subtler bug from the dead-sensor problem described earlier. Sensors that had gone silent months ago still had one row in the historical table — their last known reading, frozen at whatever timestamp they died. Left unwindowed, that frozen reading got treated as a real 24-hour interval and folded into the same hour-of-week statistical bucket, over and over, for each dead sensor on a dead street. A live audit of the forecast grid found that 102 of 239 segments were being labeled "reliable" — confident-looking cells were built entirely out of sensors that hadn't spoken in a year. The fix was to window the lookback, eventually settling on 30 days, so a dead sensor's ghost can no longer masquerade as ongoing data. The forecast is explicitly labeled a forecast.

Rewind mode, and a two-act lesson about where code actually runs

Rewind mode plays back the last seven days of bay activity like a video — drag a scrubber, watch dots flip between free and occupied across the CBD. Under the hood it's a single database function, rewind_window(), that assembles a full week of state transitions into one JSON payload the frontend can step through client-side.

The first version of that function ran in roughly 14 seconds — fine to build against, obviously too slow to ship. After optimization work (better indexing, tuned memory settings), it ran in about 2 seconds, verified directly in Supabase's SQL editor. Shipped it. It immediately started 502ing in production.

The gap turned out to be environmental, not computational. The SQL editor runs queries as the postgres superuser, with no statement timeout at all. The actual production path goes through PostgREST, connecting as a role called authenticator, which carries an 8-second statement timeout by policy. A query that comfortably finishes in the editor can still get killed mid-flight through the real API, and the failure surfaces as a generic 502 with no obvious connection to "query too slow" unless you know to look for it. "Works in the SQL editor" turned out to prove nothing at all about production behavior — a distinction that's obvious in hindsight and easy to miss the first time, because both paths run the literal same SQL against the literal same database.

That got fixed with a covering index and tighter memory scoping, landing consistently around 2.6 seconds through the real API this time, actually verified against the real API. Case closed, or so it seemed for a few weeks.

Then the sensor feed doubled — more on that in a moment — and rewind_window() slowed back down to 10 to 16.5 seconds, comfortably over the timeout again. This time the fix wasn't another round of query tuning, because query tuning is a linear response to a problem that had already proven it could jump nonlinearly overnight, with no warning, at the whim of an external data provider. The actual fix moved the expensive work off the request path entirely: a background cron job now recomputes the full seven-day payload every five minutes and stores it in a small cache table, direct SQL, no PostgREST hop, no timeout to race against. The public-facing function collapsed down to a single cached row lookup — trivially fast regardless of how large the underlying computation gets.

The night the data source doubled itself

That sensor-feed jump deserves its own telling. With no announcement, the City of Melbourne's public feed grew from 3,388 sensor records to 6,324 — an 87% increase, overnight, with nothing in any changelog to explain it.

The instinct here mattered more than any specific code change. The response wasn't to assume the new data was fine because the pipeline kept running without visibly erroring — that's exactly the kind of "looks fine, might not be" situation the whole project exists to be suspicious of. Before trusting the expanded feed, I checked it for the signature of a bulk synthetic load: a suspicious cluster of near-identical timestamps that would suggest placeholder rows rather than real hardware. There wasn't one. The new sensors' status timestamps were spread naturally across years, back to 2022, consistent with a set of already-existing sensors simply being newly exposed through the public API rather than freshly installed.

Once that was confirmed, the ingestion pipeline absorbed the expansion with zero code or schema changes, because nothing in it had ever been written to assume a fixed bay count — it just upserts whatever the feed hands it. The only real fallout was cosmetic: hardcoded copy on the About page citing "~3,388 sensors" and "half the network is dead" both needed a manual sweep, settling on "a third" for the dead-sensor fraction, since coverage had actually improved in relative terms even as the raw dead count grew. The one place the doubling did cause a real structural problem was downstream, in rewind mode — the second act of the story above.

Two incidents where "it says it succeeded" was the whole bug

Two separate incidents, months apart, both boil down to the exact failure mode the project's founding principle was written to guard against: something reported success, and it wasn't true.

The first was a cron job. Every night at 3:15am, a scheduled job was supposed to refresh the zone-level parking restriction data — which streets are 1P, 2P, loading zones, and so on. cron.job_run_details, Supabase's own status table for scheduled jobs, showed that job succeeding, every single night, for over five weeks. It hadn't actually run correctly once in that window. Buried in the job's stored command was a literal, unsubstituted placeholder string, <SERVICE_ROLE_KEY>, sitting where a real authentication key should have been, almost certainly dropped during an earlier key migration. Every nightly attempt was authenticating with that placeholder, getting rejected with a 401, and never writing a single row. cron.job_run_details doesn't actually mean "the job's logic completed successfully" — it means "the job was dispatched." Those are different claims, and the table's own name invites you to conflate them. The real signal was in a lower-level network log table, which showed the 401s the whole time, for anyone who thought to check it instead of trusting the friendlier-looking status table. Restriction hints across the entire CBD had been frozen at five-week-old values the whole time, silently, with every system-level indicator saying everything was fine.

The second was more serious. A string of database migrations, written over the course of several weeks, each aimed to lock down which functions the public — unauthenticated — role could execute, particularly a couple of ingestion functions that started with an unconditional TRUNCATE on their target table. Each migration ran a REVOKE EXECUTE ... FROM anon, and each one, checked naively, looked like it had worked. A direct verification query later proved that anonymous access to every one of those functions was still true, despite three separate migrations each believing they'd closed it. The root cause was a genuinely easy trap: Postgres automatically grants EXECUTE to a built-in role called PUBLIC the moment any function is created, and every other role — including anon — implicitly inherits whatever PUBLIC can do, regardless of what's revoked from that role by name. Revoking anon's own named grant did nothing, because anon was never using its own grant; it was riding PUBLIC's. Until that was understood and an explicit REVOKE ... FROM PUBLIC was added, the practical state of the database was that a single unauthenticated POST request with an empty JSON payload could have permanently wiped a production table. Tracing it further back turned up the actual root cause: a database-level default-privilege rule that had been silently re-granting anon and authenticated access to every newly created table, sequence, and function, automatically, at creation time — meaning the same class of bug would have kept quietly reappearing on every future migration until that default was fixed too, not just the symptom fixed once.

Neither incident was caught by anything announcing itself. Both were caught by refusing to accept a green status as proof, and running a direct query to check what was actually true underneath.

A bug that wasn't a bug

Not everything was a security incident. Some of it was just funny in retrospect. The theme toggle uses a single icon that's supposed to represent both light and dark mode — a yin-yang symbol felt like an elegant single-glyph solution, since the symbol already encodes exactly the duality the button represents. A user reported it was invisible in dark mode. The obvious hypothesis is a CSS bug: wrong color, wrong contrast, an inherited style stomping on it. None of that was true. The Unicode yin-yang character, on that particular platform, was being rendered as a fixed-color emoji bitmap by the system font — a tiny raster image, baked at build time by whoever drew that emoji, completely outside the reach of currentColor, theme variables, or any CSS rule whatsoever. It wasn't ignoring the styling; it was never a text glyph subject to styling in the first place. It took a screenshot, not a computed-style inspection, to actually see what was happening — the DOM and the CSSOM both looked completely correct. The fix was to stop asking a font to draw the icon and just draw it directly, as a small hand-built inline SVG, the same way every other icon in the app already worked. A five-minute fix, once the actual cause was visible instead of assumed.

Where it stands, and what's still open

The map is live in production today, deployed on Vercel, running against the real City of Melbourne feed on a two-minute ingestion cycle. It has three modes — live, forecast, and a full week of scrubbable history — a colour-blind-safe palette option, geolocation-aware search radius, and marker clustering that specifically clusters only the free bays, because a cluster count of unavailable spaces isn't information a driver needs and would visually crowd out the ones that are.

Some things are still genuinely open, and staying honest about the project means saying so rather than presenting a finished-looking snapshot. A handful of lower-priority hardening items — some remaining function grants on read-only endpoints, error monitoring that doesn't exist yet, a log-retention setup that still needs configuring — sit openly labeled as not done. And every one of those, notably, still runs into the same rule that shaped the rewind-cache fix above: this project treats its own database as read-only from an assistant's hands. Nothing gets written or altered directly, no matter how confident the fix — every schema change gets written out as a migration file for a human to actually read and run by hand in the database's own SQL editor. It's a slower way to work, and it's also the same underlying instinct as everything else in this post: don't let something claim to be done until a human — or a direct, un-trusted query — has actually checked.

That's a deliberate choice, and it's the same one running through everything above. Verify, don't trust turned out not to be a slogan from week one that got outgrown. It's just how the project still gets built, months later, one green status at a time refused to be taken at face value.

Built with Next.js, Supabase (Postgres + PostGIS), Deno Edge Functions, pg_cron, MapLibre GL, and Protomaps.

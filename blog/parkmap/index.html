Building parkmap.melbourne

City of Melbourne publishes vast amounts of data for urban analysis. One of those data sets holds info on the on-street parking bays scattered throughout the city.

I thought it would be useful to build something for the public that takes that data and renders a live map with history and a forecast.
The idea I had in mind answered these questions: "Where can I park my car, and can I park here on x day at y time".

Parkmap.Melbourne - A near-real-time map of on-street parking in Melbourne's CBD.

What is it?

parkmap.melbourne is a near-real-time map of on-street parking spaces in and around the Melbourne CBD. Based on live sensor data from thousands of in-ground parking bay sensors the City of Melbourne operates across the city. Click a bay and you get its status, its zone's restriction schedule, and a short list of better options nearby. Scrub backwards and you get seven days of history, played back like a recording. Look forward and you get a probabilistic forecast of today and the next day.

It's not a reservation system. It does not tell you that a bay is legally yours. It doesn't even tell you, with certainty, that a bay is free - it tells you what the most recent sensor reading says, how old that reading is, and lets you decide how much to trust it. That hedge is not a legal disclaimer bolted on afterward. It's the actual design principle the whole thing is built around, and it's there because the alternative — a map that looks confident and is sometimes simply wrong — is worse than useless. A parking app that occasionally sends you to a space that was never there doesn't get a second chance from its users.

It's also, deliberately, not a startup. No auth, no billing, no accounts. It's a portfolio piece: something built to be legible to another engineer in about five minutes, and to demonstrate that I can take a messy, half-documented data feed and turn it into something that's honest about its own limitations.

That honesty turned out to be the hardest part of the project, and not for the reason I expected. I assumed the challenge would be the map — the tiles, the dots, the interactions. The challenge was that almost everything upstream of the map was, at some point, quietly wrong, and it never announced itself as wrong, it looked fine.

Day one: Data & the API

The City of Melbourne publishes its parking sensor data as an open feed; GeoJSON, updated continuously, free to use under a CC BY 4.0 licence. Reading the field names, one looked completely unambiguous, status_description, with two possible values, "Present" and "Unoccupied". You'd reasonably assume "Present" means a car is present, i.e. the bay is occupied. You'd also reasonably assume the opposite framing — that the field is answering "is a car present in this reading," full stop, which is exactly what it does. The gotcha wasn't that the field lied; it's that reading it quickly and confidently, the way you read a field you're sure you understand, gets you the wrong answer. is_free is status_description == "Unoccupied", and if you build the query the "obvious" way, you get a map showing occupied bays as free and empty bays as taken — the single worst possible bug for a parking map to ship.

That bug never made it to production, because of something I did almost as a reflex from the very first week: never trust an HTTP 200. Check the actual values. The early build log for this project — kept as its own document, project-record.md, essentially a running list of "things that looked fine and weren't" — reads like a small museum of this exact failure mode. An export sampled from features[0] looked stale because the export wasn't sorted and the first feature just happened to be an old one. A request for more than 10,000 bays quietly 400'd instead of paginating, because the endpoint had an undocumented cap. A cron job reported success in its own status table while actually hitting the wrong Supabase project entirely. A key got pasted into a chat log and had to be rotated. A table that should have existed, according to the schema, turned out to be a phantom — caught only because I asked a tool to check, rather than assuming the migration had run.

None of these were exotic bugs. They were all the same bug, wearing different clothes: something reported success, and the actual state underneath was different. By the end of that first week the project had accumulated an operating principle, stated flatly in that early document and never really revised since:

Verify, don't trust. Every data bug this project hit looked fine at HTTP/200 and was wrong underneath.

I did not know, writing that sentence early on, how many times it would come back to be the actual moral of a story months later — including two incidents, described below, that would have been genuinely serious if that instinct hadn't been load-bearing by then.

The other founding fact from that first week: roughly half the sensor network was silently dead. Not slowly degrading — bimodal. A sensor either reported fresh data or it hadn't spoken in weeks, with almost nothing in between. That fact became a permanent constraint on the whole UI. The map doesn't report a bare percentage of free bays, because a bare percentage implies a denominator you can trust. It says "based on X of Y bays" — always. Coverage is treated as a feature to disclose, not a caveat to bury in a footnote.

The stack, and the one part I over-built

The technical shape of the project is fairly conventional: Next.js on Vercel for the frontend, Supabase (Postgres + PostGIS) for the database, Deno Edge Functions on pg_cron for ingestion, MapLibre GL for rendering. None of that is where the story is. The interesting reversal is the basemap.

Rendering a detailed vector map of a city center means either paying a per-request tile provider or hosting your own tiles. My first plan was the "proper" version of self-hosting: use Protomaps' open tile format, but put the tile archive in a Cloudflare R2 bucket, with its own CORS configuration and its own account to manage. It's the textbook scalable answer. It's also the wrong answer at this scale. The actual tile extract for the Melbourne CBD, cut down to just the area the app needs, is 7.2MB. That fits inside a Next.js app's public/ folder as an ordinary static file, served by Vercel exactly like any other asset, with zero extra infrastructure, zero extra accounts, and zero additional failure modes. The R2 plan was dropped once that became obvious — a small, satisfying instance of building the sophisticated version first and then realising the simple version was correct.

Making the map look honest

The visual design of the map went through more failed attempts than I expected, and the failures are instructive because they weren't really about taste — they were about the same "looked fine in isolation, wrong in context" pattern showing up in pixels instead of data.

The map uses a dark basemap by default, which is a slightly risky choice — a lot of design guidance treats saturated accents on a dark background as an anti-pattern, "neon on black." The first attempt at the palette picked colors that, compared swatch-to-swatch, looked like a reasonable dark theme. Rendered as an actual map, it read as solid black. The problem was the contrast ratio between adjacent elements — buildings against background measured at roughly ΔE 9, well under the threshold where a human eye reliably distinguishes them. The fix then overcorrected in the opposite direction: roads were brightened on the theory that thin lines need more contrast to read at all, which is true in isolation and disastrously wrong at city scale, because a dense street grid multiplies that brightness across thousands of line segments. The result was a glaring white wireframe that outcompeted the one thing on the map that actually mattered — the colored dots showing bay availability. The eventual fix pushed contrast for buildings up to ΔE ~23 while deliberately keeping roads restrained, and assigned parks, water, and motorways hues (olive, teal, sand) chosen specifically to never compete with the dot palette. None of this is subtle once you see it wrong in a screenshot. All of it was invisible comparing colors two at a time in a palette editor.

The same instinct shows up as an explicit rule elsewhere in the design: there is no heatmap, and there will not be one. A heatmap is tempting — it's a natural way to visualize "this area tends to be busy" — but building one means interpolating between known data points across unmeasured space, and that interpolation is, quietly, a claim. It asserts knowledge the sensors don't actually have. Given that the entire premise of the project is not overstating what the data supports, a heatmap was rejected on principle rather than difficulty. It would have been easy to build. It would also have been a lie shaped like a nice gradient.

Building a forecast.

Once enough history had accumulated, the natural next step was a forecast layer — an "arrive at 5pm, how likely is a bay nearby" view. The build order here is worth mentioning because the naive version is the one that fails first: an early implementation tried to answer this with a probe-grid approach, effectively cross-joining candidate time probes against every historical interval. It's conceptually simple and it's O(probes × intervals), which is fine until the tables grow, at which point it just times out. The replacement — duration-weighted interval-splitting, computing P(free) directly from how long each bay actually spent in each state — was strictly better on every axis: faster, simpler to reason about, and cheaper to run. It's one of the rare cases in this project where "simpler" wasn't a trade-off against something else; it was just the result of having tried the complicated thing first and learned what it actually needed to do.

The forecast layer also inherited a subtler bug from the dead-sensor problem described earlier. Sensors that had gone silent months ago still had one row in the historical table — their last known reading, frozen at whatever timestamp they died. Left unwindowed, that frozen reading would get treated as a real 24-hour interval and folded into the same hour-of-week statistical bucket, over and over, for every dead sensor on a dead street. A live audit of the forecast grid found that 102 of 239 segments were being labeled "reliable" purely on the strength of this fiction — confident-looking cells built entirely out of sensors that hadn't spoken in a year. The fix was to window the lookback (eventually settling on 30 days, matched to the database's own retention policy), so a dead sensor's ghost can no longer masquerade as ongoing data. The forecast is explicitly labeled a forecast and the live map is explicitly not — a "live difficulty" reading is a snapshot of right now, never dressed up as a prediction, because those are different epistemic claims and blurring them would be exactly the kind of overstatement the rest of the project works hard to avoid.

Rewind mode, and a two-act lesson about where code actually runs

Rewind mode plays back the last seven days of bay activity like a video — drag a scrubber, watch dots flip between free and occupied across the CBD. Under the hood it's a single database function, rewind_window(), that assembles a full week of state transitions into one JSON payload the frontend can step through client-side.

The first version of that function ran in roughly 14 seconds — fine to build against, obviously too slow to ship. After optimization work (better indexing, tuned memory settings), it ran in about 2 seconds, verified directly in Supabase's SQL editor. Shipped it. It immediately started 502ing in production.

The gap turned out to be environmental, not computational. The SQL editor runs queries as the postgres superuser, with no statement timeout at all. The actual production path goes through PostgREST, connecting as a role called authenticator, which carries an 8-second statement timeout by policy. A query that comfortably finishes in the editor can still get killed mid-flight through the real API, and the failure surfaces as a generic 502 with no obvious connection to "query too slow" unless you know to look for it. "Works in the SQL editor" turned out to prove nothing at all about production behavior — a distinction that's obvious in hindsight and easy to miss the first time, because both paths run the literal same SQL against the literal same database.

That got fixed with a covering index and tighter memory scoping, landing consistently around 2.6 seconds through the real API this time, actually verified against the real API. Case closed, or so it seemed for a few weeks.

Then the sensor feed doubled — more on that in a moment — and rewind_window() slowed back down to 10 to 16.5 seconds, comfortably over the timeout again. This time the fix wasn't another round of query tuning, because query tuning is a linear response to a problem that had already proven it could jump nonlinearly overnight, with no warning, at the whim of an external data provider. The actual fix moved the expensive work off the request path entirely: a background cron job now recomputes the full seven-day payload every five minutes and stores it in a small cache table, direct SQL, no PostgREST hop, no timeout to race against. The public-facing function collapsed down to a single cached row lookup — trivially fast regardless of how large the underlying computation gets. The goal wasn't to make it fast again. It was to stop being in a fight it could lose a third time.

The night the data source doubled itself

That sensor-feed jump deserves its own telling. With no announcement, the City of Melbourne's public feed grew from 3,388 sensor records to 6,324 — an 87% increase, overnight, with nothing in any changelog to explain it.

The instinct here mattered more than any specific code change. The response wasn't to assume the new data was fine because the pipeline kept running without visibly erroring — that's exactly the kind of "looks fine, might not be" situation the whole project exists to be suspicious of. Before trusting the expanded feed, I checked it for the signature of a bulk synthetic load: a suspicious cluster of near-identical timestamps that would suggest placeholder rows rather than real hardware. There wasn't one. The new sensors' status timestamps were spread naturally across years, back to 2022, consistent with a set of already-existing sensors simply being newly exposed through the public API rather than freshly installed.

Once that was confirmed, the ingestion pipeline absorbed the expansion with zero code or schema changes, because nothing in it had ever been written to assume a fixed bay count — it just upserts whatever the feed hands it. The only real fallout was cosmetic: hardcoded copy on the About page citing "~3,388 sensors" and "half the network is dead" both needed a manual sweep, settling on "a third" for the dead-sensor fraction, since coverage had actually improved in relative terms even as the raw dead count grew. The one place the doubling did cause a real structural problem was downstream, in rewind mode — the second act of the story above.

Two incidents where "it says it succeeded" was the whole bug

Two separate incidents, months apart, both boil down to the exact failure mode the project's founding principle was written to guard against: something reported success, and it wasn't true.

The first was a cron job. Every night at 3:15am, a scheduled job was supposed to refresh the zone-level parking restriction data — which streets are 1P, 2P, loading zones, and so on. cron.job_run_details, Supabase's own status table for scheduled jobs, showed that job succeeding, every single night, for over five weeks. It hadn't actually run correctly once in that window. Buried in the job's stored command was a literal, unsubstituted placeholder string, <SERVICE_ROLE_KEY>, sitting where a real authentication key should have been, almost certainly dropped during an earlier key migration. Every nightly attempt was authenticating with that placeholder, getting rejected with a 401, and never writing a single row. cron.job_run_details doesn't actually mean "the job's logic completed successfully" — it means "the job was dispatched." Those are different claims, and the table's own name invites you to conflate them. The real signal was in a lower-level network log table, which showed the 401s the whole time, for anyone who thought to check it instead of trusting the friendlier-looking status table. Restriction hints across the entire CBD had been frozen at five-week-old values the whole time, silently, with every system-level indicator saying everything was fine.

The second was more serious. A string of database migrations, written over the course of several weeks, each aimed to lock down which functions the public — unauthenticated — role could execute, particularly a couple of ingestion functions that started with an unconditional TRUNCATE on their target table. Each migration ran a REVOKE EXECUTE ... FROM anon, and each one, checked naively, looked like it had worked. A direct verification query later proved that anonymous access to every one of those functions was still true, despite three separate migrations each believing they'd closed it. The root cause was a genuinely easy trap: Postgres automatically grants EXECUTE to a built-in role called PUBLIC the moment any function is created, and every other role — including anon — implicitly inherits whatever PUBLIC can do, regardless of what's revoked from that role by name. Revoking anon's own named grant did nothing, because anon was never using its own grant; it was riding PUBLIC's. Until that was understood and an explicit REVOKE ... FROM PUBLIC was added, the practical state of the database was that a single unauthenticated POST request with an empty JSON payload could have permanently wiped a production table. Tracing it further back turned up the actual root cause: a database-level default-privilege rule that had been silently re-granting anon and authenticated access to every newly created table, sequence, and function, automatically, at creation time — meaning the same class of bug would have kept quietly reappearing on every future migration until that default was fixed too, not just the symptom fixed once.

Neither incident was caught by anything announcing itself. Both were caught by refusing to accept a green status as proof, and running a direct query to check what was actually true underneath.

A bug that wasn't a bug

Not everything was a security incident. Some of it was just funny in retrospect. The theme toggle uses a single icon that's supposed to represent both light and dark mode — a yin-yang symbol felt like an elegant single-glyph solution, since the symbol already encodes exactly the duality the button represents. A user reported it was invisible in dark mode. The obvious hypothesis is a CSS bug: wrong color, wrong contrast, an inherited style stomping on it. None of that was true. The Unicode yin-yang character, on that particular platform, was being rendered as a fixed-color emoji bitmap by the system font — a tiny raster image, baked at build time by whoever drew that emoji, completely outside the reach of currentColor, theme variables, or any CSS rule whatsoever. It wasn't ignoring the styling; it was never a text glyph subject to styling in the first place. It took a screenshot, not a computed-style inspection, to actually see what was happening — the DOM and the CSSOM both looked completely correct. The fix was to stop asking a font to draw the icon and just draw it directly, as a small hand-built inline SVG, the same way every other icon in the app already worked. A five-minute fix, once the actual cause was visible instead of assumed.

Where it stands, and what's still open

The map is live in production today, deployed on Vercel, running against the real City of Melbourne feed on a two-minute ingestion cycle. It has three modes — live, forecast, and a full week of scrubbable history — a colour-blind-safe palette option, geolocation-aware search radius, and marker clustering that specifically clusters only the free bays, because a cluster count of unavailable spaces isn't information a driver needs and would visually crowd out the ones that are.

Some things are still genuinely open, and staying honest about the project means saying so rather than presenting a finished-looking snapshot. The custom domain isn't attached to the deployment yet, even though the app's own metadata and sitemap already reference it — a small mismatch waiting on a DNS step. A structural fix for the rewind-performance saga, specifically designed to survive another 2-3x jump in sensor count without regressing into the same 502 a third time, is written and reviewed but not yet applied to the live database, because this project runs under a strict rule that the database itself is read-only from an assistant's hands — every schema change gets written as a migration file for a human to actually run. A handful of lower-priority hardening items — an unapplied index cleanup, some remaining function grants on read-only endpoints, error monitoring that doesn't exist yet — sit in a roadmap file, openly labeled as not done.

That's a deliberate choice, and it's the same one running through everything above. The most recent entries in this project's own internal changelog, at the time of writing, are the cache precompute that fixed rewind's second timeout and the free-bay-only clustering feature — both still framed the same way everything before them was: here's what changed, here's why, here's how it was actually verified rather than assumed. Verify, don't trust turned out not to be a slogan from week one that got outgrown. It's just how the project still gets built, months later, one green status at a time refused to be taken at face value.
